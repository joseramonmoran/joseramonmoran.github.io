**Abstract.** Firms assembling complementary inputs face a trade-off in where to source them. Distant suppliers specialize in more distinct products, but their supply disruptions are less synchronized, and when inputs are complements, failures arriving separately cost more than failures arriving together. Using Mexican customs data, we treat the COVID-19 pandemic as a natural experiment in staggered regional disruption, comparing inputs hit at different times within the same firm. Firms respond along two opposing margins: sourcing inputs from a more common set of regions, while adding backup suppliers for each. We rationalize this in a quantitative model of global input sourcing where cost draws are correlated within regions and delivery failures share regional factors. Complementarity matters only when exposure is uneven across inputs, and comparative advantage generates that unevenness. We quantify the model to ask how much a change in perceived risk explains the post-2020 nearshoring of Mexican supply chains.
